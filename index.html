🔋1. What is Energy?

DEFINITION
Energy = the ability or power to do work. It comes from the sun, wind, water, fire, electricity and food.
Easy way: Energy is like money — you can store it, spend it, or change it into another form, but you can never create or destroy it.

Two Big Families of Energy

Potential Energy = stored energy or energy of position. It is not moving but has the "potential" to do work.
Kinetic Energy = energy of motion. Anything that moves has kinetic energy.
Potential forms:
• Chemical — stored in bonds (food, coal, petrol)
• Nuclear — stored in the nucleus (uranium)
• Stored Mechanical — compressed spring, stretched rubber
• Gravitational — water in a dam
Kinetic forms:
• Radiant — sunlight, X-rays
• Thermal — heat (vibration of atoms)
• Motion — wind, flowing water
• Sound — longitudinal waves
• Electrical — moving electrons
Example: Water stored high in a dam = gravitational potential energy. When it falls, it becomes kinetic energy → spins turbine → electricity.

🔄2. Energy Flow & Energy Conversion

Energy Conversion = changing energy from one form to another. Energy is never lost — it just changes form.

Easy way: Every machine is an "energy translator". It takes one form in and gives another out — some always leaks as waste heat.

🌡️3. Laws of Thermodynamics

Zeroth Law — Thermal Equilibrium

Zeroth Law: If two systems are each in thermal equilibrium with a third system, then they are in thermal equilibrium with each other.
Thermal Equilibrium: A state where two objects in contact have the same temperature — no heat flows between them.
Example: Body (A) touches thermometer (B). Thermometer was calibrated with a standard (C). So body temp = standard temp — that's why thermometer reading is correct.

First Law — Conservation of Energy

First Law of Thermodynamics: Energy can neither be created nor destroyed. The change in internal energy equals heat added minus work done by the system.
ΔU = Q − WΔU = change in internal energy · Q = heat added · W = work done by system
Example: Car engine burns fuel (chemical) → moves car (mechanical) + releases heat. Total energy balances.

Second Law — Entropy & Direction of Heat

Second Law (Clausius): Heat naturally flows from a hotter body to a colder body, never the other way, unless external work is done.
Entropy (S): A measure of disorder or randomness in a system. In any natural process, entropy of an isolated system increases or stays the same — it never decreases.
Irreversible Process: A natural process that moves in one direction only (e.g. heat flowing hot → cold). Increases entropy.
Reversible Process: An ideal process that can go both ways without increasing entropy (theoretical).
ΔS = ΔQ / TΔS = 0 for reversible · ΔS > 0 for irreversible
Easy way: Hot coffee cools by itself — never heats up by itself. That one-way behaviour is the Second Law.

Third Law — Absolute Zero

Third Law of Thermodynamics: The entropy of a perfect crystal at absolute zero temperature is zero.
Absolute Zero: The lowest possible temperature = 0 Kelvin = −273.15 °C. At this point, particles have minimum possible energy.
Used in: cryogenics, superconductivity, quantum computing — where materials behave strangely at very low temperature.

⚙️4. Physics of Work, Heat and Power

Work

Work: Work is done when a force moves an object through a distance. In thermodynamics, work is done when a gas expands or contracts.
δW = F·dx = −P·dVWork done by a gas when volume changes

For a finite volume change: W = −∫P dV

Heat

Heat: Heat is energy transferred between two systems because of a temperature difference. No volume change is needed.
Heat Capacity (C): The amount of heat needed to raise the temperature of a system by 1 °C (or 1 K).
ΔQ = C ΔT
Specific Heat (c): Heat capacity per unit mass (or per particle, per mole, per volume). It is an intensive property.

Units: 1 cal = 4.184 J

Thermodynamic processes: Isothermal (T const), Isobaric (P const), Isochoric (V const), Adiabatic (ΔQ = 0).

Power

Power: The rate at which work is done or energy is transferred. Unit = watt (W).
P = W / tPower = Work ÷ Time
Example: Lifting a 10 kg object 2 m in 5 s → P = (10 × 9.8 × 2) / 5 = 39.2 W

Efficiency

Energy Conversion Efficiency (η): The ratio of useful work output to total energy input.
η = Work done / Energy used

🪨1. Formation of Fossil Fuels

Fossil Fuels: Fuels formed from the remains of ancient plants and marine organisms buried underground for millions of years. Examples: coal, oil, natural gas.
Fossil Fuel Formation: A natural process where dead plants and microorganisms are buried under sediment, and under high pressure and temperature, transform into coal, oil or gas over millions of years.
FuelSource MaterialTemperatureProcessProduct
CoalLand plants (peat)50–200 °CCompaction + carbon concentrationBituminous / Anthracite
OilMarine micro-organisms60–120 °CThermal cracking of kerogenLiquid hydrocarbons
Natural GasSame as oil or coal>150 °CFurther crackingMethane-rich gas

Steps of Formation

  • Raw material: ancient plants, algae, marine microorganisms. Sun's energy stored by photosynthesis.
  • Burial & sedimentation: sand, silt, clay pile up. Pressure ↑ and temperature ↑ with depth.
  • Geothermal gradient: about 25–30 °C per km of depth.
  • Time scale: tens to hundreds of millions of years.
Geothermal Gradient: The rate at which Earth's temperature increases with depth. About 25–30 °C per km.
Photosynthesis: The process by which plants convert sunlight into chemical energy stored in organic molecules.
Easy way: Fossil fuels are basically ancient sunlight trapped in carbon bonds. Heat + pressure + time cook them into coal, oil or gas.

Coal Formation Stages

Peat → Lignite → Bituminous → Anthracite

  • Peat — partially decayed plants (low P, low T)
  • Lignite — soft brown coal (more compact)
  • Bituminous — dense, black, high carbon
  • Anthracite — hardest, highest carbon, extreme P & T

As we move along, carbon % increases and volatile content decreases.

Oil & Gas Formation

  • Kerogen stage: organic matter + sediment = oil shale
  • Oil window (~60–120 °C): kerogen cracks into liquid hydrocarbons
  • Gas window (>150 °C): oil breaks into smaller methane-rich molecules
Kerogen: A waxy organic matter found in oil shale that breaks down into oil and gas when heated.
Thermal Cracking: The process where large hydrocarbon molecules break into smaller molecules under high temperature.
Oil Window: The temperature range (60–120 °C) where kerogen breaks down into liquid hydrocarbons.
Gas Window: The temperature range (>150 °C) where hydrocarbons crack into methane-rich gas.

⛏️2. Extraction Physics

FuelExtraction MethodPhysics Principles
CoalSurface & underground miningRock mechanics, leverage, gas expansion
OilDrilling, natural pressure, water/steam injectionFluid mechanics, pressure gradients, heat transfer
Natural GasDrilling, frackingGas laws, fracture mechanics, pressure flow

Coal Extraction

  • Surface (open-cast) mining: remove overburden to expose coal. Uses mechanical power & work-energy principle.
  • Underground mining: rock mechanics (prevent collapse), ventilation physics (air flow by pressure difference), explosives (rapid gas expansion).

Oil Extraction

  • Drilling: torque breaks rock; drilling fluid uses hydrostatic pressure to prevent blowouts.
  • Primary recovery: natural reservoir pressure pushes oil up (Pascal's law, Bernoulli's principle).
  • Secondary recovery: water flooding / gas injection increases pressure.
  • Enhanced Oil Recovery (EOR): steam reduces viscosity; surfactants change surface tension.
Primary Recovery: Extracting oil using natural reservoir pressure without any external help.
Secondary Recovery: Injecting water or gas to increase pressure and push more oil out.
Enhanced Oil Recovery (EOR): Advanced methods like steam injection or chemicals to reduce oil viscosity and improve flow.

Natural Gas Extraction

  • Conventional: gas is lighter, rises naturally — gas laws predict flow.
  • Unconventional (shale gas): hydraulic fracturing — high-pressure fluid creates fractures (stress–strain, fracture mechanics).
  • Flow through rock described by Darcy's law.
Hydraulic Fracturing (Fracking): Using high-pressure fluid to create fractures in rock so gas can flow out.
Darcy's Law: Describes how fluid flows through porous rock — flow rate depends on pressure difference and permeability.

🔥3. Energy Content of Fossil Fuels

Energy Content: The amount of heat energy released when a unit mass (or volume) of fuel is burned. Measured in MJ/kg or MJ/m³.
BTU (British Thermal Unit): A unit of energy. 1 BTU = 1,055 J.
FuelMain ComponentsEnergy Content
CoalCarbon + H, O, S24–35 MJ/kg
OilHydrocarbons (C₅–C₂₀)42–47 MJ/kg
Natural GasMostly methane (CH₄)35–40 MJ/m³ (~55 MJ/kg)

Why Energy Content Differs

  • H:C ratio: more hydrogen per carbon = more heat per kg (methane > oil > coal)
  • Impurities & moisture: reduce usable heat (low-grade coal)
  • State of matter: gases are less dense in volume but high energy per mass

Conversion to Electricity (Thermal → Electric)

  • Coal plant: 33–40 %
  • Oil-fired plant: 35–42 %
  • Gas combined cycle: 50–60 % (highest)

Quick Comparison (per kg)

Coal ~6–8 kWh/kg · Oil ~11–12 kWh/kg · Natural Gas ~14–15 kWh/kg

🧪4. Combustion & Thermodynamic Analysis

Combustion: A chemical reaction where a fuel reacts with oxygen to release heat energy. It is an exothermic reaction.

Fossil fuels are hydrocarbons. Burning with oxygen releases heat.

C + O₂ → CO₂ + heat
2C₈H₁₈ + 25O₂ → 16CO₂ + 18H₂O + heat
CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O + heat

Steps in Combustion

  1. Ignition — fuel reaches activation temperature
  2. Flame propagation — rapid oxidation, heat release
  3. Complete combustion — maximum heat, CO₂ + H₂O
  4. Incomplete combustion — limited O₂ gives CO, soot, less energy
Complete Combustion: Burning with enough oxygen — produces maximum heat, CO₂ and H₂O.
Incomplete Combustion: Burning with limited oxygen — produces CO (toxic), soot, and less energy.

Thermodynamic Analysis

Heat of Combustion (Enthalpy Change, ΔH_c): The amount of heat released per unit mass or mole of fuel when burned completely.
ΔH_c = Σ ΔH°f(products) − Σ ΔH°f(reactants)

Coal ~24–35 MJ/kg · Oil ~42–47 MJ/kg · CH₄ ~55 MJ/kg

Enthalpy: A measure of total energy in a thermodynamic system = internal energy + (pressure × volume).

First Law (Energy Balance):

Q_in = W_out + ΔU

In power plants: boiler → steam → turbine → generator → electricity.

Second Law (Efficiency Limit): Carnot efficiency

η_max = 1 − (T_c / T_h)T_h = combustion gas temp (K), T_c = environment temp (K)
Carnot Efficiency: The maximum theoretical efficiency of a heat engine operating between two temperatures. η = 1 − T_c/T_h.

🌫️5. Environmental Impacts & Mitigation

Impacts

  • Air pollution: CO₂ (global warming), CO (toxic), SO₂ (acid rain), NOₓ (smog, ozone depletion), particulate matter (PM)
  • Global warming: CO₂ is the largest contributor; methane leaks are even more potent
  • Acid rain: SO₂ + H₂O → H₂SO₄; NOₓ + H₂O → HNO₃ (damages Taj Mahal, crops, forests)
  • Health hazards: PM₂.₅ & PM₁₀ cause asthma, cancer; CO poisoning
  • Ash & solid waste: fly ash, bottom ash, toxic metals (Hg, Pb, As)
  • Water pollution: thermal pollution, oil spills
Acid Rain: Rain with acidic pH caused by SO₂ and NOₓ dissolving in water vapour to form sulfuric and nitric acids.
Particulate Matter (PM): Tiny solid particles (soot, ash) suspended in air that can enter lungs and cause health problems.

Mitigation Measures

  • FGD (Flue Gas Desulfurization) — removes SO₂ using limestone slurry → gypsum
  • SCR (Selective Catalytic Reduction) — converts NOₓ into N₂ + H₂O
  • ESP & bag filters — trap ash and particulates
  • CCS (Carbon Capture & Storage) — captures CO₂, stores underground
  • Cleaner fuels — low-sulfur coal, shift coal → natural gas, promote biofuels & hydrogen
  • Policy — Euro VI / Bharat Stage norms, carbon tax, Paris Agreement
  • Afforestation — trees absorb CO₂ (carbon sinks)
Flue Gas Desulfurization (FGD): A technology that removes SO₂ from exhaust gases using limestone slurry, producing gypsum as a byproduct.
Carbon Capture and Storage (CCS): Capturing CO₂ from power plants and storing it underground so it doesn't enter the atmosphere.
Carbon Sink: Anything that absorbs more CO₂ than it releases — like forests and oceans.

⚛️6. Nuclear Energy — Fission & Fusion

Nuclear Energy: Energy stored in the nucleus of an atom — the energy that holds the nucleus together.
Binding Energy: The energy required to break a nucleus into its individual protons and neutrons. Higher binding energy per nucleon = more stable atom.

The greater the binding energy per nucleon, the more stable the atom. Iron-56 (⁵⁶Fe) is the most stable.

Graph idea: Light nuclei release energy by fusion (joining). Heavy nuclei release energy by fission (splitting). Both move towards iron.

Fusion

Nuclear Fusion: Light nuclei join together to form a heavier nucleus, releasing a huge amount of energy.
²₁D + ³₁T → ⁴₂He + ¹₀n + 17.6 MeV

Other reactions: D + D → ³He + n + 3.7 MeV; D + D → T + H + 4.03 MeV; D + ³He → ⁴He + H + 18.3 MeV

Fusion Energy Calculation (Mass Deficit)

Mass Deficit (Δm): The difference between the total mass before and after a nuclear reaction. This "missing" mass is converted into energy.
E = mc²

For D + T: Δm = 0.0187 m_H, m_H = 1.6727 × 10⁻²⁷ kg

E = 2.8184 × 10⁻¹² J = 17.56 MeV (since 1 eV = 1.6022 × 10⁻¹⁹ J)

Fission

Nuclear Fission: A heavy nucleus splits into two smaller nuclei, releasing energy and neutrons.
U-235 + neutron → Kr-90 + Ba-144 + energy + neutrons

Released neutrons can trigger more fission → chain reaction.

Chain Reaction: A self-sustaining series of nuclear fissions where each fission releases neutrons that cause more fissions.
Example: 1 kg of uranium releases about a million times more energy than 1 kg of coal.

Worldwide Nuclear Power

  • ~20 % of world's electricity, ~7 % of total energy
  • 440 reactors in 31 countries; 30 more under construction
  • Zero CO₂ or smog emissions
  • Top: United States (103 reactors), France (59), Japan (53)

🏭7. Reactor Physics — Types & Design

Pressurized Water Reactor (PWR)

Pressurized Water Reactor (PWR): A nuclear reactor where water under high pressure transfers heat from the reactor core to a secondary loop, where steam is generated to produce electricity.
  • Reactor core: fuel rods produce heat by fission. Water kept at very high pressure (~155 bar) so it doesn't boil even at ~320 °C.
  • Steam generator: heat transferred to a secondary loop; the two loops never mix → no radioactive contamination outside core.
  • Turbine & generator: steam spins turbine → electricity.
  • Condenser & cooling: third loop (river/sea/cooling tower) removes heat.
  • Control rods control fission rate; water acts as moderator & coolant.

Boiling Water Reactor (BWR)

Boiling Water Reactor (BWR): A nuclear reactor where water in the core is allowed to boil directly, and the steam goes straight to the turbine.
  • No separate steam generator needed.
  • Steam goes straight to the turbine → generator.
  • Steam is slightly radioactive, so turbines are shielded.
  • Lower pressure (~70 bar), simpler design.

Steam generationBoils directly in reactor vesselSeparate steam generator

Fast Breeder Reactor (FBR)

Fast Breeder Reactor (FBR): A nuclear reactor that uses fast neutrons and produces ("breeds") more fuel than it consumes — e.g. converts U-238 into Pu-239.
  • Uses fast neutrons (no moderator needed)
  • Produces more fuel than it consumes
  • Uses liquid metal coolant (sodium)

Neutron Moderation

Neutron Moderation: The process of slowing down fast neutrons so they can efficiently cause fission in U-235. Water, heavy water, or graphite act as moderators.
Moderator: A material (like water or graphite) that slows down neutrons without absorbing them.

Criticality

Criticality: The state of a nuclear reactor when the chain reaction is steady and self-sustaining. Governed by k_eff.
Neutron Multiplication Factor (k_eff): The ratio of neutrons in one generation to the previous generation.
  • Subcritical (k < 1): neutron population decreases, chain reaction dies
  • Critical (k = 1): steady, self-sustaining — normal power operation
  • Supercritical (k > 1): neutron population increases, reaction accelerates
Easy way: k is like a "population growth rate" for neutrons. Reactors are kept exactly at k = 1 for stable power.

Reactor Design Components

  • Fuel rods: contain uranium (U-235)
  • Control rods: absorb neutrons to control reaction rate (boron, cadmium)
  • Moderator: slows neutrons (water, graphite)
  • Coolant: carries heat away (water, liquid sodium)
  • Shielding: protects from radiation (concrete, lead)
Control Rods: Rods made of neutron-absorbing material (boron, cadmium) inserted or withdrawn to control the fission rate.
Coolant: A fluid (water, liquid sodium) that carries heat away from the reactor core.

☢️8. Radioactive Waste Management

Radioactive Waste: Waste containing radioactive materials that are unstable and decay into other elements, emitting radiation.
Transuranic Elements: Chemical elements with atomic number greater than 92 (uranium). They are unstable and decay slowly.

Types of Waste

Type% Volume% RadioactivityDetails
Low-Level (LLW)90 %1 %Paper, rags, tools, clothing; short-lived; little shielding
Intermediate (ILW)7 %4 %Resins, sludges, metal cladding; needs shielding
High-Level (HLW)3 %95 %Used fuel; heat-generating; deep disposal
Transuranic1.2 %—Alpha emitters, atomic no. > 92; long-term isolation
Low-Level Waste (LLW): Waste with small amounts of mostly short-lived radioactivity — paper, rags, tools, clothing.
Intermediate-Level Waste (ILW): Waste with higher radioactivity that requires shielding — resins, chemical sludges, metal fuel cladding.
High-Level Waste (HLW): Highly radioactive waste that generates heat and requires cooling and deep geological disposal — used fuel and separated waste.

Disposal Methods

  • Near-surface disposal: for LLW — concrete vaults, trenches
  • Deep geological disposal: for ILW & HLW — 300–500 m deep, stable rock, glass-encased
  • Sub-seabed disposal: deep ocean floor sediment, 5 miles depth
  • Island disposal: deep stable formations on islands; ocean acts as barrier
  • Ice sheet disposal: waste placed in polar ice, melts its way down
  • Space disposal: send waste to deep solar orbit (theoretical)
Deep Geological Disposal: Burying high-level radioactive waste 300–500 m deep in stable rock formations to isolate it for thousands of years.

☀️1. Basics of Solar Radiation

Solar Radiation: Electromagnetic radiation emitted by the Sun due to nuclear fusion in its core. It behaves like a blackbody at ~5778 K.
Blackbody Radiation: Radiation emitted by an ideal object that absorbs and emits all wavelengths. The Sun behaves like a blackbody at 5778 K.

Spectrum Distribution

  • Ultraviolet (UV): ~7 % (wavelength < 400 nm)
  • Visible light: ~47 % (400–700 nm) — useful for vision & photosynthesis
  • Infrared (IR): ~46 % (700–2500 nm) — responsible for heating

Intensity

Solar Intensity: The power per unit area received from the Sun. At Earth's surface, clear noon ≈ 1000 W/m².

It varies with:

  • Atmospheric absorption & scattering (clouds, gases, aerosols)
  • Time of day (solar angle)
  • Latitude and season

Global average ≈ 170–200 W/m².

Solar Constant

Solar Constant (S): The average amount of solar electromagnetic radiation received per unit area at the top of Earth's atmosphere, on a surface perpendicular to the Sun's rays. S = 1361 W/m².

Varies slightly (±0.1 %) with the 11-year solar cycle and ±3 % due to Earth's orbital eccentricity.

Fate of Incoming Solar Radiation

  • ~30 % reflected back to space (albedo — clouds, ice, atmosphere)
  • ~20 % absorbed by the atmosphere
  • ~50 % absorbed by Earth's surface (land & oceans)
Albedo: The fraction of incoming solar radiation reflected back to space by a surface. Earth's average albedo ≈ 30 %.

🔆2. Photovoltaic Effect & Solar Cells

Photovoltaic Effect: The process where light (photons) hits a semiconductor and knocks electrons free, creating an electric current.
Solar Cell (PV Cell): A device that converts sunlight directly into electricity using the photovoltaic effect.
p-n Junction: The boundary between p-type and n-type semiconductors where an electric field is created, allowing electrons to flow.

How a Solar Cell Works

  1. n-type material (extra electrons) and p-type material (extra holes) are joined.
  2. The junction creates a built-in electric field.
  3. Sunlight hits the cell → photons transfer energy to electrons.
  4. Electrons flow through the external circuit → electric current.
  5. Holes move in the opposite direction.
Example: A solar panel on a rooftop — photons in, electrons out, DC electricity produced. An inverter converts it to AC for home use.

♨️3. Solar Heat Collection

Solar Collector: A device that collects and/or concentrates solar radiation to produce heat.

Flat-Plate Collectors

Flat-Plate Collector: An insulated box with a transparent cover and absorber plate that heats water/air up to ~100 °C. Common in domestic water heating.

Evacuated Tube Collectors

Evacuated Tube Collector: Glass tubes with vacuum insulation that reduce heat loss, achieving higher temperatures (~150 °C). Useful in colder regions.

Concentrating Collectors (CSP)

Concentrating Collector (CSP): Mirrors/lenses concentrate sunlight onto a receiver, reaching 300–1000 °C. Used for large-scale power generation.
CSP (Concentrated Solar Power): A technology that uses mirrors or lenses to concentrate sunlight to generate heat, which drives a turbine to produce electricity.

  • Industrial: process heat for food, textile, chemical, pharma; solar desalination; solar furnaces
  • Power Generation: CSP plants generate steam to drive turbines; hybrid systems
  • Agriculture: solar dryers for crops, greenhouses, solar pumps for irrigation
Solar Desalination: Using solar energy to remove salt from seawater to produce fresh water.

📉6. Efficiency & Losses

Solar PV Efficiency: The ratio of electrical output power to incident solar power. Typical: 15–25 %.
η_PV = (P_out / P_in) × 100 %
Solar Thermal Efficiency: The ratio of useful heat energy collected to incident solar radiation. Flat plate: 30–70 %; Concentrating: 40–60 %.
η_thermal = (Q_useful / (A × G)) × 100 %Q = heat gained, A = collector area, G = solar irradiance

Losses in Solar PV

  • Reflection losses (~4–10 %)
  • Spectral losses — photons below bandgap wasted
  • Thermalization losses — high-energy photons lose excess as heat
  • Series resistance losses
  • Shading & dust
  • Temperature losses
  • Mismatch losses between cells/panels

Losses in Solar Thermal

  • Optical: reflection/transmission, tracking errors in CSP
  • Thermal: conduction, convection, radiation from absorber
  • Storage & transport: heat loss in pipes/tanks, insulation issues
  • Mechanical: pumping power, parasitic loads (fans, motors)

Improving Efficiency

  • PV: anti-reflective coatings, tracking systems, MPPT, cooling & cleaning
  • Thermal: selective coatings (high absorptivity, low emissivity), vacuum insulation, good thermal storage
MPPT (Maximum Power Point Tracking): A technique used in solar inverters to extract maximum possible power from PV panels.

💨1. Kinetic Energy of Wind

Wind Energy: Energy obtained from the movement of air caused by pressure differences in the atmosphere (mainly due to solar heating).
E_k = ½ v²Kinetic energy per unit mass of moving air
P_wind = ½ ρ A v³ρ = air density (~1.225 kg/m³), A = swept area, v = wind speed
Key point: Power depends on the cube of wind speed. Double the wind speed → 8 times the power. Small speed changes matter a lot!
Example: Wind at 5 m/s gives 125 units; at 10 m/s gives 1000 units — 8× more power.

🚧2. Betz's Limit

Betz's Limit: The maximum theoretical fraction of wind power that can be extracted by an ideal turbine. It is 59.3 % (16/27).
C_p,max = 16/27 ≈ 0.593
P_max = (16/27) × ½ ρ A v³

You cannot extract all the wind's energy — if you did, the air behind the turbine would stop and no new wind could flow in.

Modern turbines achieve about 40–50 %, close to the theoretical limit.

Power Coefficient (C_p): The fraction of wind power that a turbine can extract. Maximum theoretical value = 0.593 (Betz limit).

🌀3. Wind Turbine Design & Working

Wind Turbine: A device that converts the kinetic energy of wind into electrical energy.

Main Components

  • Rotor blades — 2–3 long blades shaped like airplane wings (airfoils)
  • Hub — connects blades to the main shaft
  • Nacelle — housing with main shaft, gearbox, generator, brake system
  • Yaw mechanism — rotates turbine to face the wind
  • Tower — elevates turbine to stronger, more consistent winds
  • Control system — monitors wind speed, pitch angle, performance
Nacelle: The housing on top of the tower that contains the gearbox, generator, and brake system.
Yaw Mechanism: A system that rotates the turbine to face the wind direction.

Working Principle

  1. Wind blows across rotor blades → pressure difference → blades rotate (aerodynamic lift & drag)
  2. Rotor spins the low-speed shaft
  3. Gearbox increases speed (e.g. 20 rpm → 1500 rpm)
  4. High-speed shaft drives the generator → electricity
  5. Power electronics & transformer send it to the grid
P_Turbine = C_p × ½ ρ A v³

Types of Wind Turbines

HAWT (Horizontal Axis Wind Turbine): A wind turbine with blades rotating around a horizontal axis. Most common modern design.
VAWT (Vertical Axis Wind Turbine): A wind turbine with blades rotating around a vertical axis. Examples: Savonius, Darrieus.

Power Curve (important)

  • Cut-in speed: below this, no power generation
  • Rated wind speed: turbine reaches maximum output
  • Cut-out speed: turbine stops to avoid damage
Cut-in Speed: The minimum wind speed at which a turbine starts generating power.
Cut-out Speed: The maximum wind speed at which a turbine stops to prevent damage.

💧4. Energy of Water (Hydropower)

Hydropower: Electricity generated by converting the potential and kinetic energy of flowing or falling water into electrical energy.

Potential Energy

PE = m g hm = mass, g = 9.8 m/s², h = height of water level
Gravitational Potential Energy: Energy stored in water due to its height above the turbine. PE = mgh.

Kinetic Energy

KE = ½ m v²
Kinetic Energy of Water: Energy of moving water. When water falls, potential energy converts to kinetic energy. KE = ½mv².
Chain: Potential energy → Kinetic energy → Mechanical energy → Electrical energy

Power Generation

P = η ρ g Q Hη = efficiency (85–95 %), ρ = 1000 kg/m³, Q = flow rate (m³/s), H = effective head (m)
Effective Head (H): The net vertical height of water available to produce power at the turbine.

The turbine shaft is coupled to a generator, which converts mechanical energy → electrical energy using electromagnetic induction.

⚙️5. Turbines in Hydropower

Turbine: A machine that converts the kinetic and/or pressure energy of a fluid (water or steam) into mechanical energy (rotation).

Impulse Turbines

Impulse Turbine: A turbine where high-velocity jets of water strike the blades. Pressure stays constant; only velocity changes. Based on Newton's Second Law.
  • High-velocity jets of water are directed at turbine blades (buckets/cups)
  • Kinetic energy of the jet strikes blades → rotation
  • Pressure of the fluid stays constant; only velocity changes
  • Based on Newton's Second Law (rate of change of momentum = force)
  • Example: Pelton wheel

Reaction Turbines

Reaction Turbine: A turbine that uses both velocity and pressure of the fluid to turn blades. Fully submerged. Based on Newton's Third Law.
  • Uses both velocity and pressure of the fluid to turn blades
  • Based on Newton's Third Law
  • The entire blade system is submerged in water
  • ~60–70 % of turbines worldwide are reaction type
  • Examples: Francis turbine, Kaplan turbine

🏞️6. Small-Scale vs Large-Scale Hydro

Small-Scale Hydro: Hydropower projects up to 10–25 MW, usually run-of-river type, used for local/rural supply.
Large-Scale Hydro: Hydropower projects greater than 25 MW, usually storage-based with large dams, used for national grid supply.
Run-of-River Scheme: A hydropower design that uses the natural flow of a river without building a large dam or reservoir.

  • Location: Darjeeling District, West Bengal
  • Type: Small hydropower (run-of-river scheme)
  • Capacity: ~5 MW
  • River: Rangit (tributary of Teesta)
  • Developer: WBREDA · Turbine: Francis (medium head 50–100 m)

Case Study 2 — Tehri Hydropower Project (Uttarakhand)

  • Location: Tehri Garhwal, Uttarakhand · River: Bhagirathi
  • Capacity: 2400 MW (Tehri Stage-I: 1000 MW; Koteshwar: 400 MW; Pumped Storage: 1000 MW)
  • Dam: Rock-fill embankment, 260.5 m high (one of Asia's highest)

📖 All Definitions — 1 Mark Ready

Every important definition from Units 1–4 in one place. Perfect for 1-mark questions.

⚡Unit 1 — Energy Basics

Energy: The ability or power to do work.
Potential Energy: Stored energy or energy of position.
Kinetic Energy: Energy of motion.
Chemical Energy: Energy stored in the bonds of atoms and molecules.
Nuclear Energy: Energy stored in the nucleus of an atom.
Gravitational Energy: Energy of place or position — water in a dam.
Radiant Energy: Electromagnetic energy travelling in transverse waves (sunlight).
Thermal Energy: Internal energy in substances — vibration of atoms and molecules.
Electrical Energy: Energy from the movement of electrons.
Zeroth Law: If two systems are each in thermal equilibrium with a third, they are in equilibrium with each other.
First Law of Thermodynamics: Energy cannot be created or destroyed; ΔU = Q − W.
Second Law of Thermodynamics: Heat flows naturally from hot to cold; entropy of an isolated system always increases.
Entropy: A measure of disorder or randomness in a system.
Third Law of Thermodynamics: Entropy of a perfect crystal at absolute zero is zero.
Absolute Zero: 0 K = −273.15 °C — the lowest possible temperature.
Work: Done when a force moves an object through a distance.
Heat: Energy transferred due to a temperature difference.
Power: The rate at which work is done or energy is transferred. P = W/t.
Efficiency (η): Ratio of useful work output to total energy input.
Carnot Efficiency: Maximum theoretical efficiency η = 1 − T_c/T_h.

🪨Unit 2 — Conventional Energy

Fossil Fuels: Fuels formed from ancient plants and organisms buried for millions of years.
Geothermal Gradient: Rate of temperature increase with depth (~25–30 °C/km).
Peat: Partially decayed plant matter — first stage of coal formation.
Lignite: Soft brown coal — second stage.
Bituminous Coal: Dense, black, high-carbon coal.
Anthracite: Hardest, highest-carbon coal formed under extreme pressure & temperature.
Kerogen: Waxy organic matter in oil shale that breaks down into oil and gas.
Oil Window: Temperature range 60–120 °C where kerogen forms liquid hydrocarbons.
Gas Window: Temperature >150 °C where hydrocarbons crack into methane-rich gas.
Thermal Cracking: Breaking large hydrocarbon molecules into smaller ones using heat.
Primary Recovery: Extracting oil using natural reservoir pressure.
Secondary Recovery: Injecting water/gas to increase pressure and push oil out.
Enhanced Oil Recovery (EOR): Advanced methods (steam, chemicals) to improve oil flow.
Hydraulic Fracturing: Using high-pressure fluid to create fractures in rock for gas extraction.
Energy Content: Heat released when a unit mass/volume of fuel is burned (MJ/kg or MJ/m³).
BTU: British Thermal Unit — 1 BTU = 1,055 J.
Combustion: Chemical reaction of fuel with oxygen releasing heat.
Complete Combustion: Burning with enough oxygen — produces CO₂ + H₂O + maximum heat.
Incomplete Combustion: Burning with limited oxygen — produces CO, soot, less energy.
Heat of Combustion (ΔH_c): Heat released per unit mass/mole of fuel burned completely.
Enthalpy: Total energy of a system = internal energy + (pressure × volume).
Acid Rain: Rain with acidic pH caused by SO₂ and NOₓ.
Particulate Matter (PM): Tiny solid particles in air that can enter lungs.
FGD (Flue Gas Desulfurization): Removes SO₂ using limestone slurry → gypsum.
SCR (Selective Catalytic Reduction): Converts NOₓ into N₂ + H₂O using ammonia/urea.
CCS (Carbon Capture & Storage): Captures CO₂ and stores it underground.
Carbon Sink: Anything that absorbs more CO₂ than it releases (forests, oceans).
Nuclear Fission: Heavy nucleus splits into two smaller nuclei + energy.
Nuclear Fusion: Light nuclei join to form a heavier nucleus + energy.
Binding Energy: Energy required to break a nucleus into protons and neutrons.
Mass Deficit (Δm): Difference between mass before and after a nuclear reaction — converted to energy.
Chain Reaction: Self-sustaining series of fissions where neutrons cause more fissions.
PWR: Pressurized Water Reactor — water under high pressure transfers heat to a secondary loop.
BWR: Boiling Water Reactor — water boils directly in the reactor vessel.
FBR: Fast Breeder Reactor — uses fast neutrons, produces more fuel than it consumes.
Neutron Moderation: Slowing down fast neutrons so they can cause fission in U-235.
Moderator: Material (water, graphite) that slows neutrons without absorbing them.
Criticality: State when chain reaction is steady and self-sustaining (k_eff = 1).
Neutron Multiplication Factor (k_eff): Ratio of neutrons in one generation to the previous.
Control Rods: Neutron-absorbing rods (boron, cadmium) that control fission rate.
Coolant: Fluid that carries heat away from the reactor core.
Radioactive Waste: Waste containing unstable radioactive materials.
Transuranic Elements: Elements with atomic number > 92.
LLW: Low-Level Waste — 90% volume, 1% radioactivity.
ILW: Intermediate-Level Waste — 7% volume, 4% radioactivity.
HLW: High-Level Waste — 3% volume, 95% radioactivity.
Deep Geological Disposal: Burying HLW 300–500 m deep in stable rock.

☀️Unit 3 — Solar Energy

Solar Radiation: Electromagnetic radiation emitted by the Sun due to nuclear fusion.
Blackbody Radiation: Radiation from an ideal object that absorbs and emits all wavelengths.
Solar Intensity: Power per unit area received from the Sun. Peak ≈ 1000 W/m².
Solar Constant (S): Average solar radiation at top of atmosphere = 1361 W/m².
Albedo: Fraction of incoming solar radiation reflected back to space (~30% for Earth).
Photovoltaic Effect: Light hits semiconductor → knocks electrons free → electric current.
Solar Cell: Device that converts sunlight directly into electricity.
p-n Junction: Boundary between p-type and n-type semiconductors creating an electric field.
Solar Collector: Device that collects/concentrates solar radiation to produce heat.
Flat-Plate Collector: Insulated box with transparent cover and absorber plate — heats water/air up to 100 °C.
Evacuated Tube Collector: Glass tubes with vacuum insulation — achieves ~150 °C.
Concentrating Collector (CSP): Mirrors/lenses concentrate sunlight — reaches 300–1000 °C.
Sensible Heat Storage: Heat stored by raising temperature of a material.
Latent Heat Storage: Heat stored/released during phase change (melting/solidification).
Thermochemical Storage: Heat stored through reversible chemical reactions.
Solar PV Efficiency: Electrical output ÷ incident solar power. Typical 15–25 %.
Solar Thermal Efficiency: Useful heat collected ÷ incident solar radiation.
MPPT: Maximum Power Point Tracking — technique to extract maximum power from PV panels.

💨Unit 4 — Wind & Hydropower

Wind Energy: Energy from the movement of air caused by pressure differences.
Betz's Limit: Maximum theoretical wind power extraction = 59.3 % (16/27).
Power Coefficient (C_p): Fraction of wind power a turbine can extract. Max = 0.593.
Wind Turbine: Device that converts kinetic energy of wind into electrical energy.
Nacelle: Housing on top of tower containing gearbox, generator, brake.
Yaw Mechanism: Rotates turbine to face the wind direction.
HAWT: Horizontal Axis Wind Turbine — blades rotate around horizontal axis.
VAWT: Vertical Axis Wind Turbine — blades rotate around vertical axis.
Cut-in Speed: Minimum wind speed for power generation.
Cut-out Speed: Maximum wind speed before turbine stops for safety.
Hydropower: Electricity from potential and kinetic energy of water.
Gravitational Potential Energy: Energy stored in water due to height. PE = mgh.
Kinetic Energy of Water: Energy of moving water. KE = ½mv².
Effective Head (H): Net vertical height of water available at the turbine.
Turbine: Machine converting fluid energy into mechanical energy.
Impulse Turbine: High-velocity jets strike blades; pressure constant; Newton's 2nd Law.
Reaction Turbine: Uses velocity and pressure; fully submerged; Newton's 3rd Law.
Small-Scale Hydro: Up to 10–25 MW, run-of-river, local supply.
Large-Scale Hydro: >25 MW, storage-based with large dams, national grid.
Run-of-River Scheme: Hydropower using natural river flow without large dam.
